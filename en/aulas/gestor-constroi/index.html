Lesson 1 · 15 min

The builder manager

The manager who knows the process can now build her own tool with AI. That is good, and it is risky. The mentor is there so her idea becomes something useful, tested, and handed off to IT, without turning into one more hidden tool with exposed customer data.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1What changes when the manager builds
  2. 2What the mentor does and does not do
  3. 3Where AI tends to fail
  4. 4Map 3 tools with risk

Goal: explain the mentor's role and map out with the manager 3 tools they want, each with its own risk.

  1. 1/6

    Manager

    The person who knows the problem is the person who lives the process. Patrícia knows a customer goes cold when no one follows up on a quote within two days. No programmer knows that better than she does.

  2. 2/6

    AI

    Today an agent builds a prototype in hours. Building got cheap. What is still expensive is trusting what was built: data, permissions, tests, and maintenance.

  3. 3/6

    Mentor

    You. You don't build for the manager: you make the manager able to build with a method. You ask the right questions, hold the pace, and prepare the handoff to IT.

  4. 4/6

    IT

    IT is still essential. It validates anything that will touch data and critical operations, takes care of access and security, and takes over maintenance. The mentor delivers the package; IT decides whether it goes into production.

  5. 5/6

    Governance

    A named owner, a log of actions, periodic review, and an inventory of tools. It's what keeps the tool from becoming 'that thing Patrícia made that nobody knows how it works'.

  6. 6/6

    The whole combination

    A manager who knows the process, AI that speeds things up, a mentor who guides, IT that validates, and governance that sustains. Take away any piece and the rest breaks.

Before: the manager asked IT and waited. Now: the manager prototypes, the mentor guides, and IT validates before anything goes into production.

02 · The mentor's role in four ideas

Click each step.

1. Building got cheap

A manager with no programming experience can now build an app or an agent in one afternoon. The bottleneck moved from 'knowing how to do it' to 'knowing what to do, and what not to do'.

This is where the mentor comes in: turning the desire to build into a process with a beginning, a middle, and a delivery.

The market already feels the shift. According to Stanford's Digital Economy Lab (revised 08/12/2026), employment for ages 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI is 19% lower than it would have been if it had kept pace with their peers in less exposed occupations (the study does not claim a cause). Those who are starting out need mentoring; those who manage need to learn to manage agents.

At HorizontePatrícia saw a colleague build an order app in two days. She wants the same for customer follow-up.

2. You don't build it for them

The mentor's service has four parts: training the manager, following their project, supporting architecture and integration, and organizing tests, documentation, and access with IT.

If you type the code in their place, the tool has no owner the day you leave.

Rule of thumbThe manager drives the keyboard. You ask, point, and check.

3. AI makes mistakes in predictable places

AI is great at one task and fails badly at the one next to it. The mistakes that show up most in tools built by managers: secrets in the code, data open to anyone, too many permissions, made-up numbers, and forgotten exception cases.

In the Horizonte lab there are real examples of this: an API key in the browser of the order app and a WhatsApp agent that gives discounts with no cap.

A question that saves you"If this goes wrong at 10 p.m. on a Friday, who finds out and how do they shut it off?"

4. Start with the map

In the first conversation, ask the manager for the three tools they want most. For each one: what data it uses, what action it takes, and who receives the output. This already separates what is easy from what needs IT from the start.

Also ask what the team already uses on their own. At Horizonte, the inventory found four AI tools that IT didn't know about.

Risk in three colorsGreen: only reads internal data, no personal data. Yellow: uses customer data. Red: sends outside, touches money or orders.

03 · Templates for the first session

Copy and adapt. Replace the text between < > with your own.

First mentoring session script (60 min)
SESSION 1 — Tool map · manager: <name, area> · date: <mm/dd>

0–10 min  Set the ground rules
  - I guide; you build. IT validates before anything is used with real data.
  - Nothing runs with customer data until the handoff lesson.

10–30 min  Listen to the pain
  - Which task in your week would you most like to take off your hands?
  - How many times a week does it happen? How long does it take?
  - Who else takes part in it?

30–45 min  Map of the 3 tools (fill in together, table below)

45–55 min  What already exists
  - Which AI tools do you or your team already use today?
  - Does any of them receive customer data? Who in IT knows about this?

55–60 min  Agreements
  - Which of the 3 will we filter in the next session?
  - Next meeting: <date>. Task: write down 5 real cases from the process.

Color rules:
- green: only reads internal data, no personal data, output stays inside the company
- yellow: uses personal data of customers or employees (LGPD)
- red: sends outside, touches money, price, orders, or deletes something

AI already in use in the area (quick inventory): <tool · who uses it · what data goes in>
Simulation: illustrative example
  1. Quote follow-up — customers and open quotes; suggests a message; sent after approval. Yellow.
  2. Weekly sales summary — internal report with no personal data. Green.
  3. Win back an inactive customer with a discount — touches price and talks to the customer. Red.
  • Skipping the inventory: the new tool is born next to others that nobody knows about.
  • Starting with the red one: it's the most wanted and the worst for learning. Start with a yellow or a green one.
  • Promising a date before filtering the idea in lesson 2.
